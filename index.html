AI literacy and competing expectations in design education

Four interviews (two UX practitioners at SAP, two graduate students at Indiana University) about what designers value, what organizations expect, and where AI literacy fits. Students are building competency while its definition shifts around them. Faculty restrict AI in the classroom while industry expects it in practice. Read through the lens of Human Performance Technology (HPT), the misalignment is a systems problem, not unprepared people.

Values and expectations

What education values

Design programs teach process rigor, research methodology, and design thinking principles. AI tools are restricted or discouraged to protect learning integrity and prevent shortcuts that undermine skill development.

What industry expects

Organizations expect designers to ship quickly, use AI tools pragmatically, and adapt to workflows where AI is integrated into daily practice. The workplace values output and efficiency alongside design thinking.

Gilbert's (1978) BEM and Pershing's (2006) four-element taxonomy are the diagnostic tools used here to locate where the misalignment lives in the system, and to identify which levers exist for addressing it.

Three diagnostic lenses used throughout this site

Gilbert (1978)

Behavior Engineering Model

When designers and students talk about AI literacy, are they pointing at the system or at themselves? That is what Gilbert's BEM reveals. Thomas Gilbert divided attention into two rows: the environmental row (what the organization provides: information, resources, incentives) and the individual row (what the person brings: knowledge, capacity, motives). The model is used here not to diagnose a problem but to make visible where practitioners and students focus their attention. The split reveals competing values: practitioners see unclear systems while students see personal unpreparedness.

Pershing (2006)

Four-element taxonomy

James Pershing mapped interventions to four system levels: organizational, management, physical/technical, and human/social. These levels reveal where the values clash lives. In design education, AI literacy sits at the human/social level (what programs teach). But whether students develop it depends on management decisions (faculty AI policies) and whether tools are accessible at all (physical/technical). When academic values (learning integrity) conflict with industry expectations (pragmatic AI use), no single level can resolve it. Pershing's framework shows why the disconnect persists.

Holton (1999) · Cho et al. (2011)

Transfer and the performance domain

The central argument: learning is not performance. A program can teach something correctly and still fail to produce someone who can use it in practice. The transfer disconnect (between what school teaches and what industry expects) reveals the values clash. When P4 says programs produce graduates who default to ChatGPT for resources "because that's what the environment rewarded," that shows how academic incentives shape professional habits. Elwood Holton's work on performance domains is among the most cited in HPT (Cho et al., 2011), used here to reveal why the disconnect exists, not to solve it.

What the BEM reveals

Gilbert's (1978) Behavior Engineering Model divides attention into two rows. The environmental row focuses on what the organization or system provides: clear expectations (information), the right tools (resources), and aligned consequences (incentives). The individual row focuses on what the person brings: their skills and mental models (knowledge), their technical ability (capacity), and their willingness to engage (motives). The six cells map where people focus their attention when they talk about AI literacy.

What the split reveals

Practitioners and students have their own perspectives and concerns. P1 and P2 see a systems problem while P3 and P4 experience it as a personal readiness problem. Both are correct about what they observe. The issue is that these stakeholders are connected through academics and industry leadership, yet don't directly contact each other often.

This split reveals competing values. Practitioners point at the system: organizations that reduce UX to "make it pretty," leadership that treats AI as a cost-saving replacement tool, and workplace expectations that assume AI literacy without teaching it. Students point at themselves: feeling "dumb and lazy" when using AI, noticing that AI outputs are generic and repetitive, and recognizing that what programs reward (methodology, rigor, deliverable formats) is not what workplaces value.

The disconnect exists because academic programs value learning integrity while industry values pragmatic output. Faculty restrict AI to prevent shortcuts. Industry expects AI integration for efficiency. Students develop competency in one environment while its definition shifts in the other. The mismatch is not a training problem or an organizational structure problem. It is a values clash, and students are caught in the middle of it.

Early career designers who build AI literacy now (context windows, RAG, MCP, agentic workflows) will have performance gains and more opportunities than those who wait for academia to catch up. The target is moving faster than curriculum committees can respond.

Key findings

What the four participants revealed

Synthesized impressions organized by where they land in Gilbert's BEM. Not verbatim quotes, but distillations of what each participant communicated across their interview.

Environmental row

What the organization or system provides

Information

Clarity about expectations, performance signals, and what AI literacy means for the role

P1

Management treats UX as "make it pretty," rushing features out the door without proper research or design time. The AI narrative adds to this confusion, with leadership suggesting AI can replace UX headcount rather than clarifying what design value actually means in an AI-integrated workflow.

P2

Sees the AI replacement narrative as overblown hype. The actual work of understanding users, synthesizing insights, and making strategic design decisions remains distinct from what AI tools currently do. The organizational challenge is communicating this distinction clearly rather than letting the marketing cycle drive perception.

P3

Faculty policies about AI use created confusion rather than clarity. The rule was strict enough to discourage experimentation but vague enough that students felt unsafe taking any risk. After entering professional work, discovered that workplace expectations around AI are completely different from what the program communicated.

P4

Recognizes a gap between what academic projects reward (completing the assignment) and what professional practice requires (recruiting real participants, generating actionable insights). The program structure makes it easier to interview friends than to do rigorous research, and that pattern becomes a professional habit.

Resources

AI tools, time, access, and infrastructure the designer can actually use

P1

Resources exist but the organizational pressure to ship fast undermines their effective use. Time for research and iteration is treated as optional when key customers are waiting.

P2

Has access to AI tools and uses them deliberately, capping usage at about 30 percent of the workday. The limitation is not tool availability but a calibrated choice about where AI adds value versus where human judgment remains essential.

P3

Felt actively discouraged from using AI tools in coursework, with policies threatening grade penalties. This created a resource gap where professional expectations assume AI literacy but academic preparation restricts access to the very tools needed to build that literacy.

P4

Found AI tools frustrating in their current state. Every output follows the same pattern, same layout, same structure. The resource exists but its limitations make it feel more like a constraint than an asset, especially when policy restrictions prevent exploring workarounds.

Incentives

Career signals, rewards, and organizational consequences tied to AI adoption

P1

Perceives AI as a cyclical threat to designer headcount. When executives hear that AI can do design work, the career consequence is "we can save money, we don't need to hire as many UX people." The incentive structure rewards reducing costs, not preserving design value.

P2

Views the replacement narrative as a misreading of what AI changes. The real shift is that designers become consultants rather than full-time executors of every small change. The incentive question is whether organizations recognize and reward that consulting value.

P3

The academic incentive structure is misaligned with professional reality. Assignments reward methodology and deliverable formats that practicing designers dismiss as irrelevant. The career signal students receive is that doing well in school does not predict readiness for the work.

P4

Industry incentives now prioritize shipping fast and iterating based on user reaction after launch. User research has become a bonus, not a core value. The incentive is to hack features that make products addictive rather than to deeply understand user needs upfront.

Individual row

What the person brings to the work

Knowledge

Skills, training, and mental models about AI and its role in practice

P1

Holds a strong mental model of UX core value, articulating clearly what the discipline contributes beyond visual polish. The knowledge gap is not in design fundamentals but in how to communicate that value to stakeholders who reduce UX to surface aesthetics.

P2

Understands AI as a tool with specific, bounded utility. The mental model is calibrated: AI is useful for certain tasks but cannot replace the synthesis, strategic thinking, and contextual judgment that define design value. This knowledge allows pragmatic, selective adoption.

P3

Recognizes that what was emphasized in coursework (process rigor, documentation, structured methodology) is not what professional colleagues actually care about. The knowledge gap is not in UX principles but in understanding how those principles map to workplace priorities like stakeholder communication and shipping under constraint.

P4

Observes that AI-generated designs lack originality, producing repetitive patterns and layouts. The knowledge here is diagnostic: recognizing the tool's limitations. What's missing is the skill to work around those limitations or to know when not to use AI at all.

Capacity

Cognitive and technical ability to integrate AI into design workflows

P1

Capacity is not the constraint. Has years of experience modernizing legacy products and working across platform scope. The bottleneck is organizational capacity to value and resource the work appropriately.

P2

Demonstrates the capacity to integrate AI selectively, using it where it accelerates work without compromising judgment. The skill is knowing when to delegate to the tool and when to override it, which requires both technical literacy and design expertise.

P3

Capacity is constrained by the learning environment. Policy restrictions prevented the sustained hands-on experimentation needed to build real literacy. The result is entering the workplace with theoretical knowledge but limited practical capacity to use AI tools effectively under professional conditions.

P4

Has the capacity to use AI tools but questions their value given current limitations. Can navigate interfaces and generate outputs, but lacks the capacity (or the interest) to work around the tools' repetitive, generic results in ways that would make them professionally useful.

Motives

Identity, willingness, and whether AI engagement feels professionally worthwhile

P1

Motivation is shaped by anxiety about professional identity. If AI is framed as a replacement for UX work, the motive to engage with it becomes self-protective rather than exploratory. The identity threat ("magic thing that can do this") undermines willingness to adopt.

P2

Approaches AI with pragmatic detachment. Willing to use it where it helps, willing to set boundaries where it doesn't. The motive is professional effectiveness, not fear or hype. This posture requires confidence that design value is defensible regardless of tool evolution.

P3

Feels conflicted about AI use: "I feel like I'm being dumb and lazy at the same time." The tool undermines trust in their own instincts. The motive to use AI is present but tangled with guilt and uncertainty, partly because faculty policy framed AI as academically suspect rather than as a skill to develop.

P4

Willing to use AI but skeptical of its current value. The motive is instrumental (completing tasks) rather than enthusiastic. Recognizes that relying on AI for participant recruitment or content generation shortcuts the rigor that would build real research skills, but program incentives make those shortcuts rational.

Methods, limits, and citations

Corpus

Four semi-structured interviews conducted in Spring 2026 with two UX practitioners at SAP and two graduate students at Indiana University. Three completed Qualtrics responses serve as a small companion dataset. Interviews were transcribed, segmented into meaning units (MUs) following Herring's (2004) Computer-Mediated Discourse Analysis framework adapted to spoken transcripts, and coded inductively for theme, subtheme, sentiment, tone, speech act, hedging, and emotional intensity. The codebook explicitly narrows Herring's four levels to meaning and social behavior, since structure-level coding does not transfer to spoken data.

HPT overlay

Each unique theme in the corpus was mapped to one or more cells of Gilbert's (1978) Behavior Engineering Model (information, resources, incentives, knowledge, capacity, motives) and to one of Pershing's (2006) four elements (organizational, management, physical/technical, human/social systems). The mapping is interpretive and theme-level, not separately coded at the MU level.

Tools and assistance

Claude Code (Anthropic, claude-sonnet-4-6) was used under the author's direction for four tasks: (1) CMDA coding of P4's full 187-unit transcript using the project codebook; (2) generation and validation of all data files; (3) site layout, CSS, and JavaScript; and (4) data synthesis across all four transcripts to produce the aggregates and charts shown here. All coding decisions were reviewed and confirmed by the author.

Limits

This is a four-interview, three-survey project. Aggregates here are juxtapositions, not statistical claims. Inter-rater reliability was scoped out for this course project. The P4 analysis source file contains 35 of the 187 meaning units described in the original report; the full 187-unit coding used here was produced from the complete transcript. Faculty and industry leadership perspectives are outside the scope of this study.
