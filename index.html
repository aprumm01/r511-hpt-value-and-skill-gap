How does the use of AI in design practice shift perceptions of designer value?

Four interviews (two UX practitioners at SAP, two graduate students at Indiana University) asking what AI literacy means for designer value, and whether academic programs are building toward it or away from it. Read through the lens of Human Performance Technology (HPT), the gap is treated as a systems problem: misaligned levels, not unprepared people.

The performance gap

Desired state

Designers enter practice with the skills, mental models, and contextual awareness to work effectively in an AI-integrated field. Practitioners and students share a working understanding of what AI fluency means for design value.

Actual state

Students describe entering a profession where expectations are shifting faster than their preparation. Practitioners hold conflicting views on whether AI represents a structural threat to design value or a manageable evolution of the work.

Gilbert's (1978) BEM and Pershing's (2006) four-element taxonomy are the diagnostic tools used here to locate where in the system that gap lives, and to identify which levers exist for closing it.

Three diagnostic lenses used throughout this site

Gilbert (1978)

Behavior Engineering Model

When designers and students talk about AI literacy, are they pointing at the system or at themselves? That is the question Gilbert's BEM is built to answer. Thomas Gilbert asked a deceptively simple question: when a performance gap exists, where does it live (in the environment or in the person)? His answer was that most gaps are environmental. Organizations fail to give performers clear expectations (information), the right tools (resources), or consequences aligned to performance (incentives). Training people in an environment that doesn't support performance is wasted effort, what Gilbert called the leisurely theorem.

Pershing (2006)

Four-element taxonomy

James Pershing mapped HPT interventions to four system levels: organizational, management, physical/technical, and human/social. These aren't a ranking; they're a diagnostic. Which level is actually causing the problem? In design education, AI literacy lives at the human/social level. But whether students ever develop it depends on management decisions like faculty AI policies, and on whether the tools are accessible at all. A human/social gap cannot be fixed at the human/social level if the management level is blocking access. Pershing's framework keeps you from solving the wrong thing.

Holton (1999) · Cho et al. (2011)

Transfer and the performance domain

The central argument: learning is not performance. A program can teach something correctly and still fail to produce a performer who can use it. The transfer gap (between what school teaches and what industry expects) is a structural feature of program design, not a student failure. When P4 says programs produce graduates who default to ChatGPT for resources "because that's what the environment rewarded," that is Holton's transfer problem expressed as participant voice. Elwood Holton's work on HPT performance domains is among the most cited in the field (Cho et al., 2011).

What the BEM is measuring

Gilbert's (1978) Behavior Engineering Model asks: when a performance gap exists, is it rooted in the environment or in the individual? The distinction matters because they require different interventions. You cannot train your way out of an information gap, and you cannot restructure organizations to fix a motivation gap. The six cells below map the full space of causes.

Environmental rowWhat the organization or system provides
InformationFeedback, performance expectations, and clarity about what AI fluency means for this role
ResourcesAI tools, time, access, and infrastructure the designer can actually put to use
IncentivesCareer signals, rewards, and organizational consequences tied to whether AI is adopted
Individual rowWhat the person brings to the work
KnowledgeSkills, training, and mental models the designer holds about AI and its role in practice
CapacityCognitive and technical ability to integrate AI into design workflows
MotivesIdentity, willingness, and the designer's sense of whether AI engagement is worth it

The heatmap below shows where each participant's meaning units concentrate. Practitioners (P1, P2) are heavier on the environmental row. They experience the performance gap through what management communicates and what career signals exist. Students (P3, P4) are heavier on the individual row. They are preparing to enter the system and experience the gap through what they know, what they can do, and whether they feel ready. This practitioner/student split is the structural argument of the project.

What the split implies about intervention

Gilbert's (1978) central argument was that environmental deficiencies are almost always more costly than individual deficiencies, and that most organizations intervene in the wrong row. The heatmap above shows the practitioner/student split in the data: practitioners concentrate on the environmental row (information and incentives) because they experience the gap through what management signals and what the career rewards. Students concentrate on the individual row (knowledge and motives) because they are preparing to enter a system and experience the gap as a preparation problem.

The intervention logic follows directly from the cell location. For practitioners, the gap is not a training problem. P1 and P2 both have access to AI tools. What they lack is organizational clarity: clear information about what AI fluency means for a UX role, and incentive structures that make AI adoption feel professionally safe rather than threatening. More training would address the wrong row. The fix is at the information and incentives cells, which means management, not HR learning programs.

For students, the gap is not primarily organizational. P3 and P4 are not yet inside the organizations that would need to change. Their language concentrates on knowledge and motives: what they know how to do with AI, and how they feel about using it. P3's "I feel like I'm being dumb and lazy at the same time" is a motives-cell statement, not a resources-cell one. P4's frustration with AI-generated outputs that produce "the same pattern, same design, same layouting" is a knowledge-cell statement: a gap in the mental model of what AI can and cannot contribute to design judgment. These are exactly the cells an academic program can address, if it stops treating AI as a policy problem and starts treating it as a pedagogical one.

The AI moment makes this more urgent because the environmental row is changing faster than programs update the human/social row. By the time a curriculum revision passes committee, the information and incentives in organizations will have shifted again. Holton's transfer gap is not widening because students are less capable. It is widening because the target keeps moving and the program is standing still.

Methods, limits, and citations

Corpus

Four semi-structured interviews conducted in Spring 2026 with two UX practitioners at SAP and two graduate students at Indiana University. Three completed Qualtrics responses serve as a small companion dataset. Interviews were transcribed, segmented into meaning units (MUs) following Herring's (2004) Computer-Mediated Discourse Analysis framework adapted to spoken transcripts, and coded inductively for theme, subtheme, sentiment, tone, speech act, hedging, and emotional intensity. The codebook explicitly narrows Herring's four levels to meaning and social behavior, since structure-level coding does not transfer to spoken data.

HPT overlay

Each unique theme in the corpus was mapped to one or more cells of Gilbert's (1978) Behavior Engineering Model (information, resources, incentives, knowledge, capacity, motives) and to one of Pershing's (2006) four elements (organizational, management, physical/technical, human/social systems). The mapping is interpretive and theme-level, not separately coded at the MU level. Mapping rationale and the full theme-to-HPT table are in data/themes_to_hpt.json.

Tools and assistance

Claude Code (Anthropic, claude-sonnet-4-6) was used under the author's direction for four tasks: (1) CMDA coding of P4's full 187-unit transcript using the project codebook; (2) generation and validation of all data files (mus.json, aggregates.json, quotes.json, themes_to_hpt.json); (3) site layout, CSS, and JavaScript; and (4) data synthesis across all four transcripts to produce the aggregates and charts shown here. All coding decisions were reviewed and confirmed by the author. The codebook used is analysis/CMDA_Codebook_v2.docx.

Limits

This is a four-interview, three-survey project. Aggregates here are juxtapositions, not statistical claims. Inter-rater reliability was scoped out for this course project. The P4 analysis source file contains 35 of the 187 meaning units described in the original report; the full 187-unit coding used here was produced from the complete transcript. Faculty and industry leadership perspectives are outside the scope of this study.
